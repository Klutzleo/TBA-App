# STORY WEAVER GUIDE

**Tools for Being Awesome RPG v3.1**

---

# Introduction

Welcome, Story Weaver! Thank you for taking on this role. TBA is designed to make your job easier while giving you powerful tools to create memorable stories. This guide will help you run amazing sessions, create balanced encounters, and handle the unexpected moments that make tabletop gaming special.

---

# Session Preparation

## Pre-Session Checklist

Use this checklist before each session to ensure you're ready:

- Review player character sheets. Know their stats, Tethers, current DP, and active Bonds
- Know your players' Bonds and Combos. Who has a Bond with whom? What is the Combo called? What does it do? Are any characters close to Ascension?
- Prepare 3-5 key NPCs. Names, motivations, and basic stats
- Check your NPC Bonds. Have any of your NPCs fought together long enough to earn a Combo? Build it before the session, not during
- Sketch 2-3 possible scenes. Where might the party go? What might they do?
- Have a random encounter ready. In case players go completely off-script
- Review last session notes. Continuity matters! Refresh your memory
- Check "The Challenged" status. Do any players need check-ins this session?

Remember: Over-preparation is wasted effort. Players WILL go off-script. Prepare lightly, improvise heavily.

# Encounter Design

## Building a Fight with Threat Points

Every enemy has a threat value. Add them up, compare the total to your party size, and you'll know how hard the fight is going to be.

**Step 1: Find each enemy's threat.** Compare the enemy's level to your party's level (if your party is split across levels, use the average).

| Enemy level compared to the party | Threat |
|:-:|:-:|
| 3 or more lower | 1/4 |
| 2 lower | 1/2 |
| 1 lower | 3/4 |
| Same level | 1 |
| 1 higher | 1 1/2 |
| 2 higher | 2 |
| 3 higher | 3 |
| 4 higher | 4 |
| 5 higher | 5 |

**Step 2: Add them up and compare to the number of players.**

| Difficulty | Total threat | What to expect |
|:-:|:-:|:--|
| Easy | Up to half the number of players | A quick victory. A warm-up. |
| Standard | Number of players minus 1 | The party wins, with about half their DP gone. |
| Hard | Equal to the number of players | A coin flip. A healer in the party tips it your way. |
| Deadly | More than the number of players | Possible Total Party Knockout. Save it for the moment that matters. |

**Examples for 4 level 3 players:**

- **Easy (2 threat):** Four level 1 enemies. (Each is 2 levels lower, so 1/2 apiece.)
- **Standard (3 threat):** Three level 3 enemies. Or one level 5 lieutenant (2) and one level 3 enemy (1).
- **Hard (4 threat):** Four level 3 enemies. Or one level 7 boss (4 levels higher, so 4 threat).
- **Deadly (5 or more):** One level 8 boss. Or five level 3 enemies.
- **Swarm:** Lots of weak bodies. Six level 1 enemies is 3 threat. See the Swarm rules below.

One big boss and a crowd of small enemies can be the same threat. Pick which one fits your scene.

## Swarms

Match swarm stats to player stats on the Leveling Table. For example, a level one swarm will use level one stats. Swarms cannot be healed. The DP being subtracted is equivalent to the bodies in the swarm getting knocked out.

## Watch Out For These

- **Past level 10, the dice stop growing.** A level 15 enemy is only about 2 threat to a level 10 party. For Ascension fights, build by the numbers (DP, Edge, BAP), not by the level.
- **The table doesn't include BAP, Combos, or buffs.** Those make your players stronger than these numbers suggest. If you want a fight to feel dangerous, use the stats of one level higher on the Leveling Table.

## Difficulty Calibration Tips

- If combats feel too easy, add more enemies (not higher level)
- If players never reach or get close to 0 DP, encounters aren't challenging enough
- If players are getting knocked out constantly, remove an enemy before you lower levels
- In testing, a standard fight runs about 8 rounds. If yours are over in two or three, you're running easy
- The Challenge triggers at -10 DP or worse. At low levels that takes a lot of damage, so it's rare early on and dramatic when it happens
- Use environmental hazards to add complexity without adding enemies

**When to Call for Rolls**

Not every action needs a roll. Here's when to use them:

**DO call for a roll when:**

- Failure is interesting. A failed lockpick could trigger an alarm
- Success is uncertain. Jumping across a chasm could go either way
- Time matters. Searching a room while guards approach creates tension
- It would be dramatic. Convincing the king to spare a prisoner

**DON'T call for a roll when:**

- The outcome is obvious. Opening an unlocked door
- It slows pacing. Rolling to walk down a hallway
- Failure has no consequence. They can just try again with no cost

Golden Rule: If the answer is 'yes' or 'no', regardless of the roll, don't roll. Only roll when both success AND failure create interesting outcomes.

Ties go to the defender. If a roll ties, the person being rolled against wins. In combat, a tie is just 0 damage.

# NPC Creation

### Quick NPC Template

Every NPC needs three things: stats, personality, and motivation.

**Stats:** Assign IP/PP/SP (1, 2, 3), choose level, calculate DP/Edge from table

**Personality:** 2-3 traits (greedy, brave, paranoid, cheerful, etc.)

**Motivation:** What do they want? Why? (revenge, wealth, safety, knowledge, etc.)

NPCs and enemies use the same tables as the players. Give a creature a few abilities (attacks, heals, buffs, or debuffs) from the same slot rules, with dice from the Leveling Table. Same rules, same dice, no extra math to learn.

When a spell or technique attacks, the defender adds the same stat it attacked with. An SP attack is SP vs SP, an IP attack is IP vs IP. Weapon attacks are Physical vs Physical.

## Common NPC Archetypes

Use these templates as starting points and modify as needed:

**Friendly Merchant -** Stats: IP 2, PP 1, SP 3 - Personality: Greedy, cowardly, knows rumors - Motivation: Make profit, avoid danger - Use for: Quest hooks, selling items, local gossip

**Guard Captain** - Stats: IP 1, PP 3, SP 2 - Personality: Dutiful, suspicious, follows orders - Motivation: Maintain order, protect the city - Use for: Authority figure, obstacle, potential ally

**Street Urchin** - Stats: IP 2, PP 3, SP 1 - Personality: Clever, desperate, knows shortcuts - Motivation: Survive, find food, help younger kids - Use for: Guide, informant, sympathetic character

**Corrupt Official** - Stats: IP 3, PP 1, SP 2 - Personality: Arrogant, selfish, cunning - Motivation: Accumulate wealth and power - Use for: Villain, obstacle, morally gray choice

**Tavern Keeper** - Stats: IP 2, PP 3, SP 1 - Personality: Friendly, gossip, observant - Motivation: Run a successful business, stay neutral - Use for: Information hub, safe haven, quest giver

# Handling Off-Script Players

Players WILL do unexpected things. Here's how to handle it:

**"Yes, And…" Technique**

Accept what players do, then build on it. Never say 'no' unless it breaks the game.

- Player: "I want to jump off the cliff!"
- Bad SW: "No, you can't do that. You fail."
- Good SW: "Okay! Roll Physical to grab a branch on the way down…"

**Fail Forward**

Failed rolls should create new problems, not dead ends.

- Player fails to pick a lock
- Bad: "You fail. The door stays locked."
- Good: "The lock breaks! The door is now jammed, AND the guards heard the noise…"

**Flexible NPCs**

Have NPCs who can deliver key information in multiple ways.

- If players skip the merchant, the guard can give them the same clue
- If they avoid the tavern, a street urchin can overhear and share
- Don't lock critical info behind one interaction

# Random Encounter Toolkit

Always have 2-3 encounters ready to drop in when players go completely off-script:

- Combat Encounter: Bandits, wild animals, or rival adventurers
- Social Encounter: Mysterious stranger with information or a quest
- Environmental Challenge: Collapsing bridge, sudden storm, rockslide

# Table Management

## Spotlight Management

- Watch for quiet players - ask them direct questions
- Don't let one player dominate - gently redirect
- Everyone should get 1-2 moments to shine per session

## Pacing

- If combat drags, speed up enemy turns (group rolls for minions)
- Take breaks every 90 minutes - stretch, snack, bathroom
- End sessions on cliffhangers OR victories (not mid-combat)
- Balance roleplay and combat - aim for 50/50 or 60/40

## When Things Go Wrong

- Made a mistake? Acknowledge it, retcon if needed, move on
- Players arguing? Take a 5-minute break, discuss offline
- Forgot a rule? Make a judgment call, look it up later
- Session bombing? Pivot to what players ARE excited about

# Using TBA-Specific Systems

## The Challenge

> **STORY WEAVER NOTE**
> This game is made for ages 6 and up. You know your players, so adjust how heavy The Challenge feels. For younger kids, keep it light: a scare, a scar that becomes a fun story, and a big comeback. For older kids, let the stakes and the goodbyes land harder. Either way, if someone is upset, slow down and check in before you continue.

- Triggers at -10 DP or worse - this is rare but impactful
- It happens the moment they hit -10. Nobody gets a turn to heal them first
- Make it dramatic - pause, set the scene, give them time to speak
- Grant BAP for genuine emotional pleas (not just "I want to live")
- Use morality prompts to guide them if they're stuck
- SW difficulty scales with character level: 1d6 at levels 1-3, 1d8 at 4-6, 1d10 at 7-9, 1d12 at level 10+
- **At Ascension (levels 11-15):** Roll 1d12, plus 1 at levels 13-14 and plus 2 at level 15. That bonus matches the Edge they've gained since level 10, so the odds stay where they were at level 10. Treat it as a legendary moment, not a punishment. Everything they have built, every Bond they have formed, every ability they have mastered is on the line. Play it like it matters.

**How it ends:**

- **Win by 4 or more:** They keep going and return at 1 DP. A clean return. No Scar.
- **Tie, or win by 1-3:** They keep going and return at 1 DP, but they are Battle Scarred. A scar or a fear becomes a new Tether. A limitation just stays with them.
- **Lose:** They are out of the adventure. Let them narrate their final moment, and create a Memory Echo.

### The Challenged Status

Only characters who are Battle Scarred gain the status "The Challenged." A clean return doesn't count. Each time a character is Scarred, their Times Challenged count goes up by 1.

### Check-Ins - Roll 1d6 when SW determines:

- 1: Bad Dream. Take -1 to your next roll
- 2-5: Peaceful sleep. No effect
- 6: Good Dream. Gain +1 to your next roll

### Frequency:

- 1st Time Challenged: Randomly during rests (SW discretion)
- 2nd Time Challenged: During rests + randomly during adventuring
- 3rd and 4th Time Challenged: During rests + adventuring + even during battle (SW discretion)
- 5th Time: Out of the adventure. No more chances at The Challenge.

### Tips:

- Don't overuse check-ins - once per session maximum for dramatic effect
- Make check-ins meaningful moments, not random interruptions
- The escalating frequency creates mounting tension as players rack up close calls
- Some players enjoy keeping "The Challenged" status for the chance at +1 rolls. It's a fair gamble: a Bad Dream and a Good Dream are equally likely, and most nights are peaceful

### Clearing The Challenged Status:

- Complete a spiritual quest
- Visit a holy site or a powerful healer
- Perform a ritual (SW determines requirements)

Clearing the status doesn't clear the count. A fifth Scar is still the end.

## Tethers

- Grant up to 3 story Tethers per character. They evolve as the story does
- Battle Scarred, Grief, and Ally sadness are extra and don't use up those slots
- Allies can have Tethers too, the same as characters. They start with none
- Save new Tethers for MAJOR character moments (not every session)
- Let them emerge naturally, don't force it
- Examples: surviving The Challenge, making a vow, facing a big fear
- Apply bonuses/penalties when a Tether is clearly relevant
- Tethers don't use buff or debuff slots. They stack on top of everything else
- A Tether can add to or take from a Challenge roll. You decide if it fits the moment

## Memory Echoes

- Always create one when a character goes out of the adventure as a hero, and always when an Ally does
- Any other time is your call. Make one if the story wants it
- They stay special if you don't hand out too many. Three to five per campaign is a good rule of thumb
- Memory Echoes can be items, a hero's weapon, armor, or personal belonging that carries their essence
- Can create major victories, betrayals, or sacrifices
- Make them location-specific ("where Marcus made his stand")
- Story first. If you want an Echo to matter mechanically, use a Tether-style buff (+1 to +3 on the Tether scale) when the party acts on it

## Being Awesome Power (BAP)

- Reward creative, cinematic, or dramatic actions
- Not every action deserves BAP; save it for epic moments
- Examples: sacrificial plays, clever plans, emotional speeches
- Don't grant BAP for routine combat or obvious choices
- You can grant BAP on any roll, defense included. It stacks with Edge, buffs, items, and Tethers
- Enemies don't earn BAP, except through their Combos
- BAP on Combo attacks is automatic; you do not decide it, the rule grants it
- BAP is for moments. If it's showing up every round, it isn't a moment

# Bonds & Combo Attacks

Bonds are one of the most powerful narrative tools in v3.1. They reward players for investing in each other's stories and give you new levers to pull as an SW.

## Recognizing a Bond

A Bond forms when two characters have earned something real together through play. You decide when that moment happens, not a rule, not a level threshold. You feel it.

#### *Moments that earn Bonds:*

- One character pulls another back from The Challenge
- Two characters survive a fight they had no business surviving
- A shared secret, a vow under pressure, a sacrifice neither will forget
- A moment so specific to their story that only they would understand it

When it happens, say it out loud: "You two have something now. Name your move." Let them name the Combo together. It belongs to them.

## Tracking Bonds

Keep a running list of Bonds in your session notes. Before each session, review who is bonded with whom and what their Combo is called. This is your setup list for cinematic moments.

- Know the Combo name - use it when they fire it. "The Ember Slam" hits differently than "they attack together."
- Know what the Combo does - damage + damage? Debuff into attack? Heal + attack? You need to adjudicate it correctly when it fires
- Know the cost - 1 use per character, same as any ability. Uses come back every encounter, so track it the same as any other ability use
- Each named Combo can fire once per encounter. Burn it now, or save it for the moment that matters

## How Combos Fire

The proposer declares the Combo on their turn and holds; their initiative slot skips. When the acceptor's next turn arrives, the Combo fires and both roll simultaneously. Both characters add their own BAP bonus automatically.

If either partner drops to 0 DP before the Combo fires, it is cancelled, and the held action is lost. Getting hit alone does not cancel it. A cancelled Combo costs nothing and doesn't count as fired, so they can try again.

- Think of initiative as an infinite repeating list; the proposer's slot just skips each time it comes around until the acceptor's turn arrives
- Other characters still take their turns normally between the hold and the fire
- When it fires, both players roll simultaneously, announce results together for maximum drama
- Both effects land. If both hit the same stat, the stronger one takes over. That means a "two buffs" Combo only works when the buffs land on different stats or targets. They can't stack
- A Combo doesn't have to attack. A buff or debuff in a Combo counts one step higher on the buff table (+4 max). A heal is a normal roll, so it gets the free BAP like anything else, and the once-per-person rule still holds

## Enemy Combos

This is one of the best tools in your arsenal. NPCs who have fought together long enough can have named Combos just like players. Build them before the session.

The rule: you do not announce the Combo. You just hold the lieutenant's action. Let the party figure out why.

- The first time an enemy Combo lands, it should be a horror moment; they never saw it coming
- After that, the dread is the point. When they see that the lieutenant goes still, they will panic
- Let them plan around it. Let them try to prevent it. Let it succeed anyway sometimes
- Enemy Combos reward players who pay attention to NPC relationships, telegraph the Bond in the fiction before you use the mechanic
- Enemy Combos fire once per encounter too. Make it count

**Example:** The Ashen Chain Reavers run in threes. Two hold while one baits. It's not in their stat block, it's just how they fight. Build it before session one. Name it. The party will learn to fear the moment the second Reaver goes still.

## Triple Combos

Three characters, all at Ascension (level 11 or higher), all holding bilateral Bonds with each other. The proposer holds, both acceptors hold on their respective turns, and all three fire on the last acceptor's next turn.

- Cost: 1 use per character (3 total across the party)
- Once per encounter, like any Combo
- All three add their own BAP bonus automatically
- All effects stack
- Triple Combo bonus: every roll gets +2
- If any of the three drops to 0 DP before it fires, the entire Combo is cancelled

Enemies can have Triple Combos too. A three-NPC coordinated strike is devastating. Telegraph it. Let the party see it coming. Then let it land.

## The Grief Tether

When a Bond is broken by a partner being out of the adventure or leaving the campaign, the character gains a Grief Tether (-1 to -3 depending on severity) until you lift it through narrative resolution. This is not a permanent negative, but could be applied to memories of their friend, or people, places, and things that remind them of that friend.

- You set the weight based on how significant the Bond was
- It does not expire on a timer; it is a story tool, not a punishment
- Lift it when the story earns it: a ritual, a resolution, a moment of acceptance
- That moment of lifting should feel like something, make it a scene, not a checkbox
- Why not give a Grief bonus when they're facing the enemy who knocked out their friend? (+1 to +3, same range as the penalty)
- On the character sheet, it lives in the Tether section like any other persistent modifier

This mechanic makes leaving a campaign have real weight for the players who remain. A player who drops out mid-campaign now has a mechanical trace in the world. Use that.

# Ascension (Levels 11-15)

Ascension is what happens when the hero's journey is complete, and the legend begins. Your job as SW changes at Ascension; you are no longer running a story about people becoming powerful. You are running a story about legends doing legendary things.

## Secondary Effects

At each Ascension level, the next ability in the character's slot order gains a secondary effect: slot 1 at level 11, up through slot 5 at level 15. You and the player define it together. It must fit within the existing system: attack, heal, buff, or debuff. Nothing new gets invented for the slot.

- The effect should feel narratively inevitable, like what this ability was always becoming
- A fire ability might gain a Fear rider. A precision strike might gain Blind. A healing ability might also cleanse one debuff
- Secondary effects do not cost an additional use, one activation, fully realized
- They don't deal damage on their own; they enhance, not replace
- A debuff effect lands automatically on a hit, no second roll. It follows the normal rules, so a new one on the same stat replaces the old one
- On an area ability, you decide which targets it reaches. There's no map, so make the call that fits the scene
- If a character's first slot is an Ally or a Summon, the level 11 effect goes to them. The Ally (or summon) gains it whenever it hits

**Example:** Dragon's Ember at level 10 is an IP-based attack. At Ascension 1, you and Scalelord decide: every hit now also applies Fear, because after ten levels of wielding Fyrax's fire, the flame carries the dragon's will. The roll doesn't change. The damage doesn't change. But the target now knows what it is to be seen by a god.

## Ascension Combos

When the second Bond partner in a pair reaches Ascension, their named Combo evolves. This is a table event. Treat it like the moment the original Bond formed, out loud, in the fiction, not a form to fill out.

When it happens, say: "You two have been through everything. What does it look like now?"

- The players revisit the Combo together. Does the name still fit? What do the secondary effects add?
- The name is their call, not yours
- Secondary effects are bound to what each character has unlocked through Ascension; nothing new gets invented for the Combo specifically
- The Combo is not new, it is the same move, just fully realized
- Ally Combos work the same way. The Ally stays at level 10, and the Combo grows through the player's Ascension abilities
- Allies fall behind on raw numbers at Ascension, since they stop at level 10. Their Combos, and the level 11 effect if the Ally is in slot 1, are what keep them in the story. Watch it in play

## Running Level 15

Level 15 is the true ceiling. A fully Ascended character has all five abilities realized, Ascension Combos evolved, and maximum DP/Edge/BAP. They are legends.

What changes at level 15 is your job as a storyteller. These are not heroes growing into power; they are powers existing in the world. The stakes, the enemies, and the narrative weight should match.

- Enemies at this level should feel the weight of the party's history; they know who these people are
- The Challenge at level 15 is not a failure state; it is an epic. Play it that way
- Triple Combos at full Ascension should feel like the story reaching its natural climax

# Other TBA Systems

## Ally Sadness (Ally Out of the Adventure)

- Enforce when an Ally is out of the adventure. This is important for emotional weight
- It works like the Grief Tether. It starts at -1 (up to -3), and you set the weight based on how deep the bond ran
- Anyone who was Bonded to the Ally also gains a Grief Tether, and every Combo with the Ally is lost
- Work with the player to find a meaningful resolution (1-3 sessions)
- Options: time, ritual, quest, redemption act
- Don't drag it out unnecessarily, respect player agency
- Always create a Memory Echo when an Ally is out of the adventure

## Healing

Healing spells and techniques roll like any other spell or technique: spell die + stat + Edge. Each person can only be healed by a spell or technique once per encounter. Potions and rest don't count toward that. A heal always wakes someone at negative DP, and so does a potion, and nobody goes above their max DP.

After the encounter, healing is free, but the players have to say it out loud. Who are they patching up, and how? If there's no time, there's no time.

Make it fit your setting. It's the same rule, so describe it however your genre needs:

- **Fantasy:** a spell
- **Sci-fi:** a med-injector or nano-patch
- **Modern or gritty:** a field medic

## Potions and Items

Potions are rewards, not a supply line. A Tier 6 potion heals 16 DP, which is a lot against a small pool. Hand them out as treasure. Potions heal a set amount with no roll, and healing potions aren't subject to the once-per-encounter limit, so a player with a few of them has real staying power. Keep an eye on how many they're carrying.

## Buffs and Debuffs

### Buffs (No Roll Needed):

- Check what die applies according to character level or item tier
- Review the Buff/Debuff table for the bonus. Every buff lasts 2 rounds: the caster gets one action with it on their next turn, and it ends at the start of their turn after that. Casting takes the caster's turn, so they don't attack that turn
- Example: Your spell is 1d10, the table shows +2. That buff adds +2 and lasts 2 rounds

### Debuffs (Contested Roll):

- Player rolls spell die + stat + Edge vs target's 1d6 + the same stat + Edge. A tie goes to the target
- If the player wins, the debuff applies for 2 rounds
- Example: Player rolls 1d10 (gets 8) + IP 3 + Edge 2 = 13 vs Enemy 7. The 1d10 debuff is -2, lasts 2 rounds

### Buff/Debuff Table:

- 1d6: +/-1
- 1d8: +/-2
- 1d10: +/-2
- 1d12: +/-3
- 2d6: +/-3
- 2d8: +/-4

**Tips:**

- Work with players to define effects (speed up, blind, make stronger, make weaker)
- Keep it simple; most should affect attack/defense rolls
- Every buff or debuff is tied to a stat (IP, PP, or SP): the one used by the roll it changes. A character can have one buff and one debuff on each stat at the same time, and both apply. A new buff on a stat replaces the old one, and the same goes for debuffs
- If an effect doesn't change a roll (speed, blind, and so on), you pick the stat that fits
- Combos are the exception that makes this fun. Both effects land
- A flat bonus applies to each die of a multi-die weapon, just like Edge. BAP is the exception: it goes on one die

**Common effects.** These aren't extra moves a player asks for. They're what your buffs and debuffs look like. When a spell or technique blinds, stuns, or slows someone, it's a debuff or buff like any other, and the number from the table is the size. A BAP moment can earn a **BAP effect**: +1 or -1 for 1 round (until the start of your next turn). If you throw a pie in someone's face and the Story Weaver gives you BAP for it, they can also say it blinds them with a -1. The Story Weaver picks the stat, and it follows the same one-per-stat rule as other buffs and debuffs. Start with these so every table plays it the same way:

- **Blind:** debuff on the stat they attack with (Physical for weapons, Intellect for most magic)
- **Slowed or hasted:** Physical debuff or buff. Also change how far they can move this turn, your call
- **Stunned or dazed:** Physical debuff
- **Confused or afraid:** Intellect debuff
- **Charmed, scared off, or silenced:** Social debuff
- **Damage Effects (poison, burning, freezing, etc.):** damage over time, not a stat debuff. The target takes the spell die once at the start of their next turn and can resist it for half, like a hazard

No debuff takes someone out of the fight. A debuff makes things harder, it doesn't skip a turn, and there's one per stat, so a boss can be shaken, but not locked down. Damage Effects such as poison, burning, freezing, etc. are different. They do damage, so they can take you to 0 or below, all the way to -10 and The Challenge. That's the risk.

## Environmental Damage

The world is a threat, too. When players enter a hazardous environment, set the severity tier and roll damage. Players may resist with a relevant stat roll.

- PP (Physical): heat, cold, pressure, poison gas, exhaustion
- IP (Intellect): psychic hazards, disorienting environments, mental pressure
- SP (Social): crowd panic, social pressure, morale-based hazards

**Tiers:** Tier 1 (1d6) mild hazard → Tier 5 (2d8) world-ending conditions. You set the tier. The roll is the damage. Keep it fast.

**Avoiding:** If a character can see a hazard coming, they can try to get around it. You decide if it's possible and what it takes. If they can't avoid it, they can still resist it.

**Resisting:** The player rolls 1d6 + the relevant stat + Edge against your hazard roll. Beat it or tie it and they take half the damage (round down). A hazard isn't an opponent, so they can reduce the damage but not dodge it once it hits.

**Tips:**

- Save Tiers 4 and 5 for deadly moments. A Tier 5 hazard averages 9 damage, which is more than a whole level 1 pool, and over half of a level 5 pool, every single round
- A successful resist halves it, so don't be shy about big hazards when the party has a way to deal with them

## Vehicle Combat

Vehicles use the vehicle's Edge from the Vehicle Leveling Table instead of the character's Edge. A vehicle's DP matches the player character row for its level.

### Crew Stations:

- Gunner: Ship's Weapon Die + PP + Vehicle's Edge (attack)
- Defense Officer: Ship's Defense Die + PP + Vehicle's Edge (defend)
- Captain: 1d6 + SP + Vehicle's Edge (add to attack OR defense)
- Pilot: 1d6 + IP + Vehicle's Edge (add to attack OR defense via positioning)
- Engineer: 1d6 + IP + Vehicle's Edge (restore vehicle DP)

**Tips:**

- Unmanned stations don't function, except defense (see below)
- When someone attacks an open vehicle, meaning you can see the rider to attack them directly, ask: the vehicle or the rider? An attack on the rider hits the rider. An attack on the vehicle hits its DP
- Open vehicles are bicycles, motorbikes, and horses. On enclosed vehicles, damage goes to the vehicle DP, not the crew, unless you rule that an attack breaches the hull or targets the crew
- Size is for flavor and story, not stats. A bike and a battleship at the same level have the same numbers. Give a big ship the level that fits
- To stop a vehicle without destroying it, let players sabotage it, hack it, or call a shot. A hit knocks out one system or its movement for 2 rounds, and it costs no DP. You pick what goes offline
- A ship with nobody on defense still gets its own Edge (no stat added), so an empty ship is easier to hit but not a pushover
- At 0 DP, the vehicle is disabled; at -10 DP, catastrophic failure is imminent
- The Captain's and Pilot's bonuses are a one-time add to that roll. They aren't buffs and don't use a buff slot
- Be flexible with stats, physical for fighter pilots might make sense
- A person on foot can't win by trading blows with a same-level vehicle. Give them a way in: sabotage, hacking, boarding, or terrain

**Final Tips for Success**

- Rule of Cool: If it's awesome and doesn't break the game, allow it
- Collaborative Storytelling: You're building a story together, not fighting players
- Consistency > Perfection: Apply rules fairly, even if you make mistakes
- Ask for Feedback: Check in after sessions: what worked? What didn't?
- Have Fun: If you're not enjoying it, something needs to change
- Use AI Tools: Consider AI assistants like Claude for story arcs, plot twists, NPCs, or encounter ideas

Now go forth and weave stories. You've got this.
